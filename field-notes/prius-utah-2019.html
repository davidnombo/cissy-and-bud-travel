Field Note 01 · Prius V · November 2019

The Trip That
Started It All

A family invitation, a cancer recovery and the Utah parks that opened our eyes to a different way of traveling.

Cincinnati → Denver → Utah → Las VegasCassie + David + Spencer
In this Field NoteThe invitationThe road becomes the tripUtah on footTraveling simplyWhat changed

The invitation

In November 2019, our adult son Spencer proposed a one-way national-park journey from Denver to Las Vegas. We decided to drive the Prius V from Cincinnati and meet him there—just for fun.

For Cassie, the trip promised something more important than a collection of parks. She was recovering from cancer treatment and beginning to feel her energy return. Spending uninterrupted time as a family sounded perfect. So did the chance to prove that she could walk, hike and participate in an adventure again.

The Prius was not a camper. It provided transportation, seats, storage and room for a cooler and snack bag. At night, we stayed in small hotels, Airbnbs and little cabins. That simplicity turned out to be one of the trip’s great gifts.

The road becomes the trip

Before this journey, a road trip usually meant getting to the destination efficiently. This time, we had enough time to let the drive matter. We researched oddities and scenery, stopped for photographs and moved from one small discovery to the next.

Near Estes Park, elk stood in a residential neighborhood while children came home from school. The Prius looked tiny. We hiked through early snow near Gem Lake and took our first photograph of a national-park wilderness toilet—because apparently that deserved documentation too.

Those stops changed the emotional shape of the mileage. The drive did not feel grueling or empty. It felt like the beginning of the adventure.

Utah on foot

From Denver we crossed I-70 toward Moab. The national parks introduced us to landscapes we had never experienced—and physical challenges that made the journey personal.

Delicate Arch

The hike to Delicate Arch was hot, exposed and harder than we expected. Near the end, an exposed ledge amplified Cassie’s height anxiety, which had intensified during cancer treatment. Spencer helped keep us moving. Reaching the arch, and making the family photograph beneath it, turned “we tried” into “we did it.”

Bryce Canyon

David was unsettled by the height at Bryce’s rim. Cassie loved the color, hoodoos and paths below. Hiking down was the fun part; climbing back out was the bill that came due later. Cassie prefers a hike where the hard part comes first.

Capitol Reef and the drive between worlds

Capitol Reef produced an honest family disagreement. David loved the stark, nearly lunar terrain. Cassie and Spencer found it ho-hum, even with the petroglyphs. That difference belongs in the story: the same landscape does not have to reach everyone in the same way.

The drive through Glen Canyon was windshield magic. Roads passed between vast rock forms that seemed to change geology by the mile. By then we were also photographing the overland vans and trucks common in Utah but still rare back home. A vehicle we could sleep in felt like a dream. We did not yet know these were baby steps toward making it real.

Scout Lookout

The hike toward Scout Lookout in Zion was punishing. Before halfway, Cassie wanted to turn back. Spencer kept encouraging us, and Cassie knew that if she stopped, the family would probably stop with her. So she continued.

At the top, Cassie rested with her face in her hands while David and Spencer looked toward Angels Landing. Chipmunks—accustomed to finding hikers with granola bars—ran up her side, across her back and down again. We had packed too few snacks, but we survived. More importantly, Cassie had completed a hike that made her returning strength tangible.

We were not simply seeing a view. We were discovering what Cassie could do again.

Traveling simply

Daily life was uncomplicated. We finalized the next day’s plan each evening and kept backup options in case Cassie needed rest. We ate packed lunches or local food, slept indoors and carried layers for cold mornings and warmer afternoons.

Cassie was newly vegan, which made road food a small puzzle. At one nearly empty shoulder-season town, a barbecue restaurant may have been the only open choice. A baked potato, green beans and granola bars got the job done. The cooler immediately proved its value.

We also learned that having “all our stuff” did not require very much stuff. Doing laundry on the road reduced the need for a new outfit every day. Carrying layers meant we could adapt without returning to a room. Long hours in the Prius gave us time to talk with our adult son about yoga, life and everything else.

What changed

Dropping Spencer at the Las Vegas airport was bittersweet. We were sad to end that part of the trip, but filled with family memories—and Cassie and David still had more road ahead.

The trip sparked Cassie’s love of national parks. It proved that cancer treatment had not ended her ability to hike, travel and imagine an active future. It taught both of us that the road could be more than the space between destinations.

We would eventually build a bed in a minivan, then move into PourHouse with a refrigerator, bathroom and off-grid power. But the essential lessons arrived in the Prius: carry less, keep food close, make room for weather, let the drive become the trip, and begin with the vehicle you already have.

We wanted more national parks, more road time and more of the freedom we had found together. We did not yet know what shape that would take. We only knew that this was the trip that started it all.
