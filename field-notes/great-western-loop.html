Field Note series · Minivan · July–August 2023

The Great
Western Loop

Forty-two nights, roughly 8,000 miles and the summer our homemade camper stopped being an experiment.

Cassie + DavidThree-part working draft

At the time, it could not have been greater—or grander.

The Great Western Loop may not always sound like our biggest journey. We have since gone farther. But in the summer of 2023, 42 nights and roughly 8,000 miles in a homemade minivan camper felt almost unimaginably large.

Part I · Learning the Van

Our first night near Paducah immediately exposed a mistake. We had booked an off-grid site during extreme heat and discovered we could not sleep comfortably in the van without air conditioning. A call to the host and $90 in cash moved us into a converted barn apartment—with an outdoor walk to a shared porta-potty.

The next night, at a KOA near Memphis, a briefly open door invited hundreds of mosquitoes into the van. David wanted to go home. Cassie, covered in bites, insisted we continue. We named it Mosquitogeddon.

The trip changed as we gained altitude and the weather cooled. Santa Fe and Meow Wolf helped establish a rhythm. New Mexican food taught us to order red and green chile “Christmas.” YouTube and Atlas Obscura increasingly guided us toward experiences chosen not only to see, but to photograph and turn into art.

Bisti Badlands

At Bisti, seven miles of washboard road made us wonder what would happen if the minivan broke down. We used a visible pole in the parking area as a navigation anchor and explored only as far as we could keep it in sight. Off-grid campers surrounded us. The scale of the landscape—and the limits of our vehicle—began a quiet conversation about what might come next.

By Four Corners, we knew the trip would succeed. The bed worked. Campgrounds, food, hygiene and the schedule all worked. We remained alert to wildlife, unfamiliar roads and new cultural settings, but we were healthy, safe and spending our days laughing.

Part II · The West Opens Up

At a private campground near Mesa Verde, the owner looked at our Chrysler and said car camping was not allowed. David offered to set up the still-boxed tent, then explained that the van had been remodeled into a tiny RV. The owner laughed, looked through the window and let us stay—without ever inspecting it.

A ranger-led tour of Cliff Palace made Mesa Verde physical: ladders, climbing and the astonishing intimacy of entering protected ruins under careful rules. At Black Canyon of the Gunnison, a hot half day carried us along the South Rim Drive toward Painted Wall before we continued to Taylor Park.

Taylor Park added altitude, friends and a lesson in hydration. Bourbon, limited food and too little water produced a nearly sleepless first night for David and borrowed oxygen. Water, food, no alcohol and an early bedtime made the next night much easier.

The Yellowstone and Tetons stretch became intentional rest. Grand Prismatic Spring looked exactly as saturated and impossible in person as it does in photographs—without filters or tricks. We saw the major sights, saved our energy and continued north.

Three passes through Glacier

Glacier felt truly wild. A three-day Going-to-the-Sun Road pass let us drive the full road repeatedly, changing our timing to see different light and choosing different stops each day. We walked toward Hidden Lake Overlook and hiked roughly two miles out and back on the exposed Highline Trail.

Construction left miles of the western road unpaved. The minivan became awesomely filthy.

We saw our first glaciers and learned they may disappear within decades. Banff and Jasper began calling hard. Leaving the park, we briefly joined locals gathered around a bear cub killed by a vehicle. They told us the body would go to the tribe for appropriate processing; their greater concern was an injured mother nearby. We retreated to the safety of the van.

Part III · Saying Yes to the Detour

Near Baker, we met Steve Bodi, a serious hobby paleontologist working in the Hell Creek Formation. He invited us to a dig. We initially said no because we needed to reach Sturgis on schedule. Almost immediately, we knew we had made a mistake.

We called Steve, extended the trip by a week and built a loop through Theodore Roosevelt National Park, the Custer area and Devils Tower before returning to Baker.

Theodore Roosevelt gave us one of the best wildlife days of the trip. Light rain cooled the landscape. We saw pronghorn, elk, a coyote, an unidentified wild cat and a bison that crossed very close to the minivan. At Devils Tower, our KOA campsite sat beneath the monument and turned the evening into a photo playground.

Back at the dig, we helped clean and prepare parts of a Triceratops Steve had discovered the previous summer. Cassie climbed into a giant bucket of plaster. Beneath a tarp in a shallow pit, the fossilized frill was unmistakable—the kind of shape we expected in a museum, not in the ground in front of us.

What we proved

We took a playful road home. The Corn Palace was fun because it remained a working event space, though the surrounding experience felt commercial. In Sac City, Iowa, David photographed everything with “Sac” in the name and visited the world’s largest popcorn ball. Coming from Big Bone Lick, the route supplied enough place-name humor to keep us entertained.

North Dakota gave the minivan a rock-damaged windshield. Years later, we still had not replaced it. It became part scar, part souvenir.

The minivan experiment had succeeded.

The sleeping system was a home run. We knew how to use campgrounds, eat from a cooler, decide when to cook, hide from weather and keep moving through hardship. We returned resilient, ready for adventure and already planning upgrades. More minivan travel was not a question. It was next.
