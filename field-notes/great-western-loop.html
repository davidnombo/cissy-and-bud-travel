Field Note series • Summer 2023

Great Western Loop

Forty-two nights in a homemade minivan camper. At the time, it could not have been greater—or grander.

Field NoteGreat Western Loop

The Great Western Loop was not a vacation in a smaller vehicle. It was an experiment: could the minivan become a safe, comfortable, financially efficient home for a long journey?

The answer did not arrive all at once. It survived an off-grid mistake near Paducah, hundreds of mosquitoes outside Memphis, altitude at Taylor Park, a dead battery near Denver, a cracked windshield in North Dakota, wildlife, weather, and a spontaneous extra week built around a Triceratops.

Series status: The complete long-form working draft and interview notes are preserved in the canonical Google Doc. This polished web edition is an editorial overview while Cassie’s full interview and final fact-checking remain open.

Part One: Cincinnati to Four Corners

The first night exposed how much we still had to learn. We had booked an off-grid Hipcamp near Paducah in punishing heat. Without air conditioning, sleeping in the van was impossible. A call to the host and ninety dollars in cash moved us into a converted barn apartment—still with a walk outside to a shared porta-potty.

Then came the Memphis KOA. After a hot day around Beale Street, the briefly open van door admitted hundreds of mosquitoes. David collected a dozen bites. Cassie got hundreds. “Mosquitogeddon” nearly sent us home, but Cassie insisted that the trip continue.

Cooler air and higher elevation changed everything. In New Mexico, we settled into a cadence with food, luggage, campgrounds, and the budget. We explored Santa Fe and Meow Wolf, learned to order New Mexico chile “Christmas,” and began using YouTube and Atlas Obscura with greater intent. We were no longer only visiting places; we were finding images and experiences we could turn into art.

Bisti Badlands became a turning point. Seven miles of washboard felt long in the minivan. We used a visible pole near the parking area as a navigation anchor and stayed within sight of it while exploring. Other off-grid rigs made the limitations of our setup visible. At the same time, photographing our own van in that huge landscape made it a signature: if the van was there, we were there.

Shiprock, Four Corners, and the surrounding tribal lands made us aware of culturally meaningful boundaries that a road traveler can cross without fully understanding. Buying fry bread from roadside vendors offered a small, personal experience beyond looking at a monument. By Four Corners, the early failures no longer defined the expedition. We were healthy, safe, eating well, laughing, and confident that the system worked.

Part Two: Four Corners to Glacier

At Mesa Verde, a private campground owner initially refused our reservation because he saw people sleeping in a car. David offered to unpack the new tent and set it up, then asked him to look at the remodeled minivan as a tiny RV. The owner laughed and let us stay—without ever inspecting it.

A ranger-led tour of Cliff Palace challenged Cassie with ladders and climbing while keeping the experience manageable. The unusual intimacy of entering the ruins, moving through them, and imagining the community that occupied the landscape made the park feel radically different from a museum.

Black Canyon of the Gunnison was a hot half-day of overlooks on the South Rim Drive. Taylor Park was a larger test. We arrived at altitude underfed, underhydrated, and added bourbon with friends. David barely slept and needed borrowed oxygen. The next day brought water, food, no alcohol, and an earlier bedtime. The lesson was effective.

The Tetons and much of Yellowstone became deliberate recovery days, saving energy for Glacier. Grand Prismatic Spring looked exactly as impossible as the photographs—from the boardwalk and the overlook, in every direction. Glacier then became one of our favorite parks. We drove Going-to-the-Sun Road three times, changing our timing and light, walked toward Hidden Lake Overlook, and took an abbreviated but exposed Highline Trail hike.

Construction left the minivan magnificently dirty. More importantly, we saw our first glaciers and learned they might not remain for future decades. Banff and Jasper began calling hard.

Part Three: Glacier to Cincinnati

Near Baker, Montana, we met Steve Bodi, a serious hobby paleontologist who invited us to help at a dig. We initially said no because friends were waiting in Sturgis. Then we realized we had made a giant mistake. Steve had a narrow opening before the season ended, so we extended the trip by a week and built a loop around his timing.

Theodore Roosevelt National Park delivered one of our best wildlife days. Light rain cooled the air. A bison crossed close to the car, and we saw pronghorn, elk, a coyote, and an unidentified wild cat. At Devils Tower, the KOA put our campsite nearly beneath the monument, turning the evening into a photo playground.

Back at the dig, Steve was excavating a Triceratops found the previous summer. We brushed away dust, helped with cleanup, and worked around bones still in the ground. Cassie jumped into a giant bucket of plaster. Beneath a tarp in a shallow pit, the animal’s frill was unmistakable—the kind of shape we expected behind museum glass, not emerging from the earth under our hands.

Afterward, we chose a playful route home: the Corn Palace, every “Sac” sign we could photograph in Sac City, Iowa, and the world’s largest popcorn ball. The van reached Cincinnati with rock damage in the windshield that we still had not replaced years later—a scar and souvenir.

We returned knowing the experiment had succeeded. The bed worked. We could eat from a cooler, cook when needed, hide from weather, manage campgrounds, adapt to altitude, and press through problems. We had upgrade ideas and no doubt the minivan would be deployed again.

We knew more travels were coming. We knew we were resilient and ready for adventure.
